# The Unpredictable Man

*Draft 1, humanizer pass applied. To the brief. Working title; alternatives: "Where the Story Starts," "Debugging My Temper."*

---

I want to start with the version where I look bad, because it is the true one, and because every other version I have told started too late.

Here is the moment. My own kitchen, a weeknight I had the kid, my phone face-up on the counter with a message thread on it that would not close. A settled thing about a handoff time, reopened, and then reopened again. Nothing in it a reasonable person would call important. And I came apart. Not at anyone in the room, because the other half of the conversation was in a different house, behind a screen. I came apart at a phone. Voice too loud, then much too loud, a cupboard door shut hard enough to jump the plates, the whole performance of a man who has lost the thread over a pickup time. There was someone in the next room who was too young to be hearing any of it, and who went very quiet, which is the detail I think about most. Said back to me the next morning, flat, the whole thing sounded like nothing, because it was nothing.

If you had been in the house, you would not have sided with me. You would have sided with the quiet in the next room. I have been in that house every night since, and most nights I side with it too.

For a long stretch of years the word that attached to me was unpredictable. Fine one day, a bonfire the next, and no way to tell in advance which one you were going to get. I did not fight the word very hard, because from the outside it was accurate. You could not predict me. You put in the same coin some days and got a smile and other days got the crater, and if that is all you can see, unpredictable is the honest word for it.

I build software for a living, and the word sat wrong with me in a specific professional way. Not because it was unkind. Because it claimed there was no pattern, and I have never in my life met a system with no pattern. When a program fails "randomly," it is not random. It is failing on an input you have not identified yet. A bug you cannot reproduce is not a bug without a cause; it is a bug whose cause you have not isolated. "Unpredictable" is what you call a machine before you have found the variable. It is a confession of ignorance dressed up as a description of the machine.

So I did the only thing I actually know how to do. I stopped defending myself and started logging.

I went back over the blowups I could reconstruct honestly, years of them, and I looked for what they had in common. The obvious suspects went first. Was I more likely to detonate when I was tired? A little, maybe, at the margins. Under work stress? Hungry, sick, stretched thin? Some, but nothing that would earn the word explosion, nothing you could build a prediction on. Was it the size of what was being asked? No, and this was the part that made me look genuinely unwell: some of the worst ones were over the smallest things. A tiny request and a disproportionate ruin.

Then I found it, and it was almost insulting how simple it was after fifteen years of paying for it.

The blowups did not track my state. They tracked one thing, and one thing only: what happened to my answer.

When I said yes, nothing ever happened. A yes closed the loop and the evening went on. When I said no, one of two things followed. Either the no was accepted, and again, nothing happened, no matter how much the no had cost me. Or the no was not accepted, and what came back was not the question again. It came back as a charge. Not about the handoff anymore, about me: the kind of father I was, the kind of man. Quiet in tone, the kind of thing that would read as plain concern to anyone but the one it was about, and it found the single thing I have never in my life been able to let lie unanswered. Then it would come again. And somewhere in there, the thing I am ashamed of would arrive, on time, every time.

The variable was never me. Not my mood, not my character, not some internal weather blowing in off nowhere. The variable was what happened to a no.

Once you have the variable, the rest of the machine starts to show its parts, and it turned out other people had already found most of them and given them names.

The first strange thing was that the other person and I could never agree on when a fight had started. To me it began the fourth time a settled question got reopened in the thread. To them it began the moment I finally blew up. Two psychologists in the 1960s had a word for this. They called it punctuation: a single unbroken loop that each person chops into cause and effect at a different place, so that each of us is sincerely, provably reacting to the other. Neither of us was lying. We were starting the tape in different spots. I had the footage for my start point. They had the footage for theirs. The two reels did not overlap by a single frame.

The second thing was a lopsidedness in how the whole sequence got read. My shouting, from the outside, was character: this is the kind of man he is. The reopening of a closed question, from the inside, was circumstance: I only asked. And I want to be exact here, because the trap is symmetrical and I was standing in the other half of it. My shouting, to me, was circumstance, look at what was happening to me. The pushing, to me, was character. Both of us read our own part as a fair response to the other's nature, and neither of us could see the machine we were jointly running, because you cannot see a loop from inside your own arc of it.

The third part was the one that rearranged the furniture. A psychologist named Gerald Patterson spent years recording families, second by second, and what he described I recognized on sight. In a certain kind of repeated fight, both people are being trained, at once, by the same events. Pressure gets applied; the other person gives in; the giving-in rewards the pressure. Or pressure gets applied and the other person finally erupts; the eruption ends the pressure, and the ending rewards the eruption. One loop, two people, both on the hook, neither choosing it exactly.

And then the piece that explained why it never, ever wore out. If a no reversed every single time it was pushed, pushing would be a reliable lever, and reliable levers are strangely easy to give up once they stop paying. But a no that gives way only sometimes, unpredictably, is not a lever. It is a slot machine. Skinner's people worked this out with pigeons a long time ago: a reward that arrives on an unpredictable schedule produces the most stubborn behavior there is, the kind almost impossible to extinguish. So every time I held a no and then, ground down, let it collapse into a yes, I was not simply losing that round. I was paying out the exact jackpot that guaranteed the machine would be fed again, and again, and for years.

The explosion itself, the part I would most like to have been a choice so I could take clean credit for stopping it, turns out to be the least chosen thing in the whole sequence. Past a certain point of arousal the body does something measurable and dumb. The heart climbs, the chemistry shifts, and the equipment that handles judgment and patience and taking in new information gets quietly powered down in favor of the older wiring that only knows fight or run. The people who study couples have a name for it. They call it flooding. It is not an excuse. It is a description, and the difference matters, because a description tells you where the repair has to go. By the time I was shouting, the machinery that would have stopped me was already offline. Which means the only place I could ever have prevented it was upstream. Earlier. Minutes or hours before, while I was still someone worth trusting with the decision.

There is one more piece, and it is the cruelest bit of engineering in the whole thing. The pushing leaves no mark. It is quiet and deniable, a worry about the kid, a question about the kind of father I am, the sort of thing that reads as reasonable to anyone who was not standing where I was standing. The explosion leaves a crater. So the record, the thing everyone remembers, the thing that is genuinely true, is the crater. My reaction became the offense, and the sequence that produced it evaporated, because it was never visible enough to record. On the tape everyone kept, I was simply a man who went off over a schedule. That tape is real. It is just cut to begin in the wrong place.

Here is where I lose you if I am careless, because here is where it starts to sound like a defense, and it is the opposite of one.

Every part I just described took two people, and I was unmistakably one of them. The loop did not run despite me. It ran on me. My caving was the jackpot that kept the machine alive; if I had never once let a held no fall over under pressure, there would have been nothing there to train. My explosions were the other half of the payout, the dependable ending that let all that pushing feel like it was getting somewhere. I was not a bystander to a pattern that happened to me. I was a load-bearing wall. You cannot build this particular trap with one person, and I carried my half of the materials to every single round of it.

And the charge that came back always landed on the one place I keep undefended. That is the fact I could turn into my entire defense, and it is exactly the one I have to refuse. An accusation that finds your worst fear is still only an input. What I built out of it, every single time, was mine.

So I would like to tell you that once I understood all this, the temper lifted like a fog. It did not. Understanding a flood does not teach you to breathe underwater. The fuse is exactly as short as it always was. The only thing that changed is that I stopped handing it matches.

If the explosion is decided upstream, before the flood, then upstream is the only place the work does anything. Not in the moment. Never in the moment. The moment is where I have already lost. So I moved the decisions to the one place I could still be trusted to make them, which is earlier, in daylight, on paper, when nobody is flooded. A no, once given, now stands by a rule set in advance, so there is nothing to reopen at midnight and nothing to defend live, because the answer was never mine to relitigate in the dark. I stopped negotiating anything that mattered in real time, which is easier when the whole thing already runs through messages: nobody has to answer the moment the phone lights up. I learned my own tell, the exact point where I can feel the equipment starting to go, and I built the exit before I needed it, set down in advance, so that ending an exchange is a rule already agreed to and not an insult I have to invent while the pressure climbs. The thread has a stop on it. Either of us can use it, and using it means nothing except that the day is done.

None of this is willpower. I have almost none of that in the moments where it would count, and I have stopped pretending otherwise. It is closer to scheduling. The calm version of me is the only one worth letting near a decision, so I let him make them and I take them out of the hands of the version who can't. For years I kept trusting the man in the flood to do better next time. He was never going to do better. He is not that guy. The only person who could ever help was the one holding the pen the day before.

I do not have a cleaner ending than that, and I have learned to be suspicious of the ones on offer. There is no scene where I am cured. The wiring is the wiring. Some nights I can still feel the old sequence reach for me, the settled thing coming unsettled, the pressure starting its slow climb, and I know precisely how short the drop is from there to the person I do not want to be in front of anyone.

I did not win anything. There was never anything to win. I just stopped scheduling the fight, and the fight, with nowhere left to happen, mostly stopped happening. The fuse is still there in the wall. I stopped wiring it to the switch.
