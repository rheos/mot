# The half of the model that was about me

I built a file to stop thinking about a person.

There was a conflict I could not leave, the same argument in slightly different clothes, and every night after the house went quiet it ran itself in my head. I would re-derive what happened, re-sort who was owed what, test a sentence that might finally make it land. An involuntary court kept convening, no jury, no adjournment. So I opened a document and wrote it down, on the theory that a thing on paper is a thing your brain can stop holding.

The danger in that is the whole reason the file was worth keeping. A private document about someone you fight with is one keystroke from being a shrine to your own grievance. You collect the evidence that fits, leave out the parts where you were the problem, and the calm font makes the contents feel like findings. So I wrote one rule at the top before anything else. If this can never be wrong, it is not a model, it is a grudge with better typography. If the predictions start missing, I revise it or I burn it.

That rule is the only reason I can tell you what I found. The most useful thing in the file turned out to be the half that was about me.

---

Here is the fight, stripped of anything that would tell you who we are. There is a thing I decline. The no is not the end of it. The request comes back, reframed, then again, the pressure climbs, and at some point I go off. I fire the message I should not have sent, and the noise of that ending is the only part anyone remembers. In the story that gets told, I am unpredictable, fine one week and a monster the next. What it leaves out is that the difference between the weeks is not my mood. It is whether the answer was yes or no. A yes was easy. A no started a slow siege.

For a long time I thought we were arguing about who was right about the events. It took an embarrassing while to see we were not describing the same events. She starts the tape at the moment I explode. I start it at the third time she asked. Both tapes are real footage, and where you start it decides who started the fight. Family therapists call this punctuation, the way each person chops a continuous loop into cause and effect so the other one always moved first. It is not a lab result, just an old and accurate name.

Under it sits something sturdier. When someone else is sharp with me, I read it as who they are. When I am sharp, I know exactly which awful afternoon produced it. Psychologists have called this the correspondence bias for fifty years, and it keeps replicating because it is true, we grade other people on character and ourselves on circumstance. So her harsh words are, to me, a personality; mine are a bad Tuesday. And it runs identically the other way. Neither of us is lying. That is the part that took me years.

There is a tidier version of this that got taught for decades and then fell apart. The neat claim was a law: everyone always explains their own behavior by the situation and everyone else's by character. When somebody added up the studies, the law came out to roughly nothing, and it was overturned. What survives is much narrower, and unluckily for me it is our case exactly. The asymmetry holds only when the two people are close and the event is a bad one, which is a fight between intimates to the letter.

---

Now the part that is mine.

For a long time I filed my explosion under provocation. She pushed, therefore. What I did not want to look at is that the explosion is not a decision I make at the top of the stairs. It happens to my body slightly before I arrive.

There is real biology under this, and getting it right is what keeps it from being an excuse. You may have heard the phrase amygdala hijack. It is a decent on-ramp and not quite what happens. The better account comes from Amy Arnsten's work on the prefrontal cortex, the part that lets you pause and hold onto the other person's view while you are angry at them. It is the region most sensitive to stress in the whole brain. Push arousal past a point and it gets chemically down-regulated, and the wheel passes to older, faster machinery built for threat, not nuance. Gottman named the felt version: flooding, the moment you stop taking in new information and everything narrows. People throw around a heart rate for it, a hundred beats a minute. I would soft-pedal that number. It is a rule of thumb, not a threshold, and the mechanism is real even where the number is loose.

And I have a short fuse to start with. That regulation machinery runs thin in an ADHD brain, so I reach the flooded state faster and climb down slower. That is not a diagnosis I am hiding behind. It is a tilt in the odds, a thing to manage, not a sentence to serve.

Here is the line the whole thing turns on. A mechanism explains the behavior. It does not license it. The nervous system floods, that happens to me. But contempt is a choice, and the cruel sentence I pick while flooded is still a sentence I picked. Flooding is weather. What I do in the weather is mine.

---

So if the explosion is partly my body, whose fault is the loop.

The honest answer, the one it took the file to see, is that the loop is not a thing either of us does to the other. It is a machine with two engines, and each of us built our own.

Sometimes a no of mine held. Sometimes, worn down, it collapsed into a yes. From outside that looks like kindness. From inside the machine it is a reward on no fixed schedule, and a behavior that pays off only sometimes is the hardest thing in the world to extinguish, because a dry spell is indistinguishable from the usual wait. Every no of mine that eventually caved did not teach her to stop asking. It taught her that asking works if you keep at it. I trained the pressing. Not with a plan. With fifteen years of intermittent surrender.

And the pressing trained me back. Gerald Patterson spent his career filming families second by second and named a coercion cycle, where two people teach each other to escalate because escalation is what ends the miserable exchange, and whoever's outburst finally stops the bout is rewarded by the silence. I have to be careful here. Patterson built it watching parents and children, so stretching it onto two adults is reasonable but not the original evidence, and coercion sounds like an accusation when it is meant as plain mechanics. It is not a verdict. It is a trap with two seats. My eruption ended the pressure, and relief is the most reliable teacher there is, so the eruption got reinforced too.

That is the beat the file was walking toward and did not want to reach. I did not get dragged into a loop. I am one of its two engines, and I had been feeding mine the whole time.

---

Which brings me to the thing I kept demanding, and why it was never going to come.

For years I wanted one sentence. Not an apology for everything. One small true thing. "The pushing is what gets me there. That one was mine." A lot of the file, if I am honest, was a case for why I was owed it. And the hardest thing it taught me is that I was aching for something the other engine cannot manufacture, which is not the same as her withholding it out of spite.

Keep this to what is documented about people in general. Some process a fault as guilt, I did a bad thing, and guilt has an exit: you name it, mend it, come out lighter. Some, when the fault gets close, feel it not as I did a bad thing but as I am bad, and that is a different animal. June Tangney's research traces how that flavor of shame does not lead to repair. It leads to escape, and when escape is blocked it turns outward as blame. A person built that way does not experience a request for acknowledgment as a request. They experience it as an attack, and the self defends by reaching for the story where the other one is at fault. Asking for the sentence does not summon it. It arms the counter-story against you.

And once a verdict like that is in place, it does not fall out when you disprove the thing that put it there. You may have read about the backfire effect, the claim that correcting a false belief makes people hold it harder. It largely failed to replicate. Nyhan, the researcher most tied to it, has since called it rare, and I would rather be accurate than dramatic. The real machinery is quieter and worse. Belief perseverance, from Lee Ross and his colleagues, showed that a belief outlives the evidence that made it, because while it was alive the person built their own reasons, and those keep standing after the original prop is kicked away. Add motivated reasoning, Ziva Kunda's work, where we reach the conclusion we need and assemble a case that feels, to us, fair. So she is not lying when she says she was the wronged one. She has done the reasoning, and it delivered a verdict she experiences as objective. I could win every fact and lose the war.

One more piece turned this from a complaint into an assignment. George Loewenstein's hot-cold empathy gap is the finding that the calm version of you cannot model the flooded version. Every calm morning I promised to stay level next time, and every calm morning underestimated the man who shows up at the top of the stairs. That is why it cuts toward me. If the cold me keeps being wrong about the hot me, the responsible thing is not a better promise. It is to stop relying on the promise at all.

---

So the exit was never her acknowledgment. That was one engine begging the other to shut off. The exit is the fuel, the only part of the machine I control.

James Gross lays out where you can step into a feeling, early to late, from choosing and changing the situation you walk into, down to white-knuckling your face after it has already flooded you. The late move is the expensive one, and it is where I had been living, promising to be strong in the exact moment my brain is least able to be. Clamping down then is not useless, but it taxes memory and relationships and leaves the thing intact underneath. The cheap move is upstream, before the feeling gathers speed. You do not win the fight at midnight. You arrange, in daylight, for there to be no fight at midnight.

Concretely, that meant a rule agreed in advance, in writing, when both of us were calm. A no that stands, because it was already settled that a declined request is closed. Nothing to push against, so nothing to keep pushing, so the pressure never climbs, so I never flood. And this is not me being strong. Peter Gollwitzer's work on implementation intentions is the plain science of it: an if-then decided ahead of time fires almost on its own when the moment arrives. The economists of self-control, Thomas Schelling among them, call this a commitment device. Ulysses lashed to the mast because he knows the song is coming and his live self will steer for the rocks. You spend the willpower in advance, when it is cheap, and take the discretion away from the midnight self who was always going to squander it.

I did not ask the flooded man at the top of the stairs to be better. I built a house he could not detonate, and did not hand him the matches.

---

There is a child in this. There has been the whole time, just off the edge of every sentence, which is where she belongs and not one inch further onto the page. She is the only reason any of it is worth doing. A kid should not grow up inside a loop, learning that this hum of low warfare is what love between grown-ups sounds like. Taking my hands off my own engine is the one move that makes it quieter in the house I control. That is not cold. It is the warmest thing left, once you understand the fight was never going to be won, only fed or starved.

I still have the file. The predictions keep coming true, the one thing I would have been glad to be wrong about. But I read it differently now. The half about her I mostly leave alone. The half I actually use is the half that was about me.
